# About ENSO Ready

## Why this exists

El Niño and La Niña change rain and temperature across much of the world. Together they affect billions of people. They bring floods to some places and drought to others. Farmers, coastal families and city residents all feel it.

The forecasts already exist. Agencies such as NOAA and WMO publish them every month. But they are written for experts, in technical bulletins, mostly in English. Most people never see them.

ENSO Ready takes those public forecasts and records and turns them into a short briefing for your own place. It says what to expect, when, and what you can do. It uses plain words, in your language where we have it.

## Who it is for

- Anyone who wants to know what this season may bring where they live.
- Farmers deciding when and what to plant.
- Families in areas that flood or run dry.
- Teachers, local officials, aid workers and radio presenters who need a short outlook they can share.

You do not need to know anything about climate science to use it.

## Who built it

One person built and runs ENSO Ready as an independent, open-source civic project. It is not part of any government, UN agency, company or charity. No organisation has endorsed it.

It has no funding. If it ever takes money, that money will come from grants only.

The code is public under the MIT licence. The way each briefing is built is described on the [methodology page](/methodology). Every number in a briefing links to its source.

## What it is not

ENSO Ready is not a warning service. It cannot tell you what will happen on a given day. For warnings and emergencies, follow your national meteorological service. Every briefing links to it.

## What it will never do

- No ads.
- No tracking, no analytics, no cookies.
- No accounts and no paywall.
- No selling or sharing of your data.

Your place and settings stay in your own browser. The site only talks to the internet for four things: its own pages, the place index, the seasonal forecast from Open-Meteo when you open a briefing, and map tiles from OpenStreetMap if you tap the map. The [privacy page](/privacy) has the details.

## Machine-translated languages

Some languages were translated by a machine and have not yet been checked by a native speaker. Those pages show a banner saying so, with a link to suggest a fix. Every sentence in a briefing comes from a fixed template. So a translation error only needs fixing once.

## How to help

**Check a translation.** If you speak one of our languages and something reads wrong, tell us. Review by native speakers is what moves a language from "machine-translated" to "reviewed".

**Review the science.** If you work in climate science or seasonal forecasting, we would value your view on the regional pattern table and the method. Say what is wrong and what source you would use instead.

**Share the briefings.** NGOs, schools, clinics and community radio stations may copy, print and read out briefings. Each briefing includes a short version written to be read aloud. Keep the date, the source line and the note about your national weather service.

**Report a problem.** Anything wrong, confusing or broken: open an issue on GitHub.
