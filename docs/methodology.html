How this works

ENSO Ready turns public climate data into a plain-language seasonal outlook for any place on Earth. It is independent and open source. It is not a warning service.

Where the facts come from

  • Current El Niño / La Niña status: NOAA Climate Prediction Center, ENSO Diagnostic Discussion, checked monthly.
  • Which past events were strong: NOAA's Oceanic Niño Index. An event is five or more overlapping seasons at or beyond ±0.5 °C; strong means a peak of ±1.5 °C or more.
  • What rain did here in past events: GPCP v2.3 monthly rainfall on a 2.5° grid, 1979 to present, compared with a 1991–2020 normal. For each grid cell and each three-month season we compute the average percentage change across the strong events since 1979, and how many of those events were wetter than normal.
  • The known regional pattern: a reviewed table of 44 regions summarising the established El Niño and La Niña impacts described by NOAA, IRI (Columbia University), the Australian Bureau of Meteorology and WMO, each with a confidence rating.
  • Official warning sources: national meteorological services, via the WMO Members directory.

How a briefing is built

  1. Your place is matched to its 2.5° grid cell and to the regional pattern it falls in.
  2. The pattern gives the expected rain and temperature signal, its season, and its hazards.
  3. The local rain record for that season is checked against the pattern. If they agree, confidence goes up; if not, the briefing says so.
  4. Confidence is expressed in three words only: likely, leaning that way, or uncertain.
  5. Preparation steps are picked from a reviewed checklist by hazard and by what you do for a living.

Every sentence is a fixed template filled with numbers from the steps above. No text is generated freely by an AI model, so every fact can be traced to its source.

What this cannot tell you

  • It cannot say what will happen on a particular day. Only your national weather service issues warnings.
  • Seasonal signals are tendencies. Even in a strong El Niño some places behave differently, and the briefing shows when past events disagreed.
  • Grid cells are about 250 km wide. Mountains, coasts and cities vary within a cell.

Privacy

No accounts, no cookies, no analytics. Your place and choices are stored only on your device.
